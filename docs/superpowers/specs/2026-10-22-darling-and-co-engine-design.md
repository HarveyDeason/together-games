# Darling & Co. — the rules engine

Design agreed with Harvey, 2026-10-22. This is project two of three. Project one
(saved games and the rooms list) is merged. Project three is trading and
auctions, and bots come after that.

## Intent

Harvey and Shruti are long distance: Keynsham in Bristol, and Nagpur in
Maharashtra. The site exists so they can play on their phones. Monopoly has been
the goal from the start, and the two games already shipped were scaffolding to
prove the architecture.

Asked what the good part looks like, Harvey said: *"we're talking in the chat,
making trades, buying houses and laughing, maybe little funny animations on
different things."* That sentence is the brief. Trading is the next project, but
everything here should be aimed at that evening.

The game is called **Darling & Co.** and never Monopoly. The rules of a board
game are not copyrightable; the name, the board design and the branding are, and
this site is public. The decks are **Lovely Jubbly** and **Jiggly Boo**.

## Goals

- Two people can start a game on their phones and play it to a finish, with
  rent, houses and jail all working.
- The game survives both of them closing the app, on the saved-games machinery
  that already exists.
- The whole board is readable at once on a phone, with no camera and no
  pinching.
- The board is swappable: Harvey supplies the real place names once it works,
  and that is a data edit, not an engine edit.
- The state leaves room for trading to be built on top without reworking it.

## Non-goals

- **Trading and auctions.** Explicitly the next project. An unowned property you
  decline is simply not bought — it is not auctioned.
- **Bots.** After trading.
- **More than two seats.** Still two, but see the constraint below: the engine is
  written as a list indexed by seat so a third is a data change.
- **Correspondence play.** They play live, sat together. No nudges, no push
  notifications, no turn timers.
- **Final board copy.** Placeholder names and card text now, written properly
  with Harvey in one pass once the game is functional.

## Anti-goals

Things that would make this a failure even if every rule worked.

- **A wasted centre.** richup.io is the reference and it throws the middle of the
  board away. Harvey looked at an isometric board and a card-first layout and
  rejected both in favour of forty squares flat with the centre doing the work.
- **A game that never ends.** Two-player Monopoly famously doesn't. Money sloshes
  back and forth, nobody goes bankrupt, and at eleven at night someone closes the
  tab. A game they abandon is a game that failed.
- **Anything forced on a player.** When you cannot pay, the game must not seize
  your houses or declare you bankrupt for you.
- **Generic copy.** Jokes about airline food will not land. The personal material
  will.

## Constraints

### The game contract — the thing the architecture rests on

Every game is one file in `src/games/` exporting `meta`, `createInitialState`,
`applyAction`, `getStatus`, `nextStarter`, `render`. `applyAction` is **pure**:
no network, no DOM, and **no randomness generated inside it**. The die arrives in
the action, generated by the caller. Both phones run the same action on the same
state and agree. This is not negotiable and it is what makes the rules testable
without a browser.

`src/main-room.js` applies actions inside a `runTransaction` on
`rooms/<code>/game`, so `applyAction` may be re-run on a retry against a
different `current`. It must therefore depend on nothing but its arguments.

### Firebase, which will bite you

- **Keys whose value is `null` are dropped.** A state written with `via: null`
  comes back `undefined`. Compare with `== null`, never `=== null`. This has
  caused two bugs in this project already. Prefer sentinel values over nulls in
  the new state: `-1` for "no seat", `''` for "nothing", `false` for "no".
- **Arrays with holes do not survive.** The Tic Tac Toe board is a nine-character
  string for exactly this reason.
- **An object whose keys are `"0"`, `"1"`, `"2"`… comes back as an array.** This
  is the trap that is new to this game: a natural `{ [squareIndex]: owner }` map
  will arrive at the other phone as a sparse array and lose its holes. Do not
  key anything by square index.
- **`firebase-rules.json` is a union across all games and is NOT deployed by
  pushing.** Harvey edits nothing; the builder edits the file and Harvey pastes
  it into the Firebase console and presses Publish. **Until he does, writes to
  unknown keys are rejected silently.** Every new field under `game/state` needs
  an entry, and `$other: { ".validate": false }` is already there to reject the
  rest.
- The `moves` cap in the rules file is 5000. A Monopoly game will not approach
  it, but check it still covers the counter as used here.

### The environment

- Harvey's terminal is **PowerShell 5.1**, where `&&` is a parse error. Give him
  `;` or separate lines.
- The dev server is `python devserver.py 5070`. Chrome refuses some ports.
- **Verify by playing it.** The two worst bugs this project has had — every board
  joke silently dropped, and the animation replaying on the winning move — both
  passed the entire unit suite. Unit tests are necessary and nowhere near
  sufficient.

## What was decided, and why

### How they actually play

Sat together, live, over an hour or two. Not correspondence — Harvey: *"i think
we'll be sat together, it wont be like chess or something where people take so
long to make moves."*

The consequence for the engine: **a decision may wait indefinitely for the other
player.** A rent demand sits there until she pays it. No timers, no auto-pay, no
forfeits. Saved games are the safety net for a dead battery or someone being
called away, not the normal mode.

### The middle of the board

Three treatments were mocked up at phone size and Harvey chose **B, the running
story**: the middle holds a short feed of the last few things that happened, a
line each, with whatever must be decided right now pinned underneath it.

*The recommendation was C, a card that flies in and lands over the dice; Harvey
chose B and that is settled.* One thing carries over from C by agreement: **the
animations still happen out on the board** — the token walking square by square,
a house popping up, money flicking across to whoever it is owed to. B describes
the turn in words; it is not where the game is animated.

The feed must not become a second chat. It narrates mechanics — who rolled what,
who bought what, what a card said — while the chat sheet stays where they talk.

The mockups are in `.superpowers/brainstorm/` and are worth looking at before
writing the renderer. `.superpowers/` is gitignored.

### What a tile looks like

- **Ownership tints the whole tile** in the owner's seat colour — `--seat0`
  coral, `--seat1` teal — not a stripe in a corner.
- **Each colour set gets a small icon as well as its stripe**, so a set is still
  recognisable when the name is four pixels tall.
- Houses and a hotel show on the tile. Mortgaged shows on the tile.
- Both tokens are always visible, including when they share a square.

### The rules

Standard Monopoly except where stated. £200 for passing GO. Doubles roll again,
three doubles sends you to jail. Rent doubles on a complete colour set with
nothing built on it. Houses, then a hotel. Stations and utilities as usual.

**Jail.** Pay £50, play a get-out-of-jail card, or wait three turns. Rolling
doubles releases you and you move that roll.

**Free Parking is a jackpot.** Fines and taxes pile up in the middle and whoever
lands on Free Parking takes the lot. Harvey's house rule, asked and confirmed.
It makes paying tax feel like something rather than just a subtraction, and it
is a second money source that shortens games.

**Mortgaging is in**, and it earns its place precisely because trading is not in
this project: with no one to sell to, mortgaging and selling houses back are the
only ways to raise cash. Unmortgaging costs the mortgage plus ten per cent.

### When you cannot pay

Harvey: *"i think she should be able to sell things at her own pace, and then
there will be a bankrupt option which will then end the game."*

So owing more than you hold puts you in a state where you raise money at your own
pace — sell houses back, mortgage properties — with nothing forced and no timer.
A **Declare bankruptcy** button sits there the whole time. Pressing it ends the
game and the other player wins.

This has a quiet benefit worth protecting: **bankruptcy is declared, never
computed.** The engine never has to decide whether a player could theoretically
have paid, which is a genuinely hard question once trading exists.

### Ending it by agreement

The real risk to "played it to a finish" is not a bug, it is the eleven-o'clock
abandon. Harvey's answer: *"maybe a button to say tally up current value, so it
adds up everything you own."*

So either player can **offer to tally up**, and **the other has to accept.** The
mutual part is the load-bearing half: a unilateral button is one the player in
front presses the moment they are ahead. On acceptance the game ends, every
holding is added up — cash, property, houses — and the bigger pile wins.

*My call:* a mortgaged property counts at its mortgage value, because that is
what it is worth to you.

### The engine's shape

- **Players are a list indexed by seat**, never hard-coded `pos0`/`pos1`. This is
  how a third seat and later a bot get added without a rewrite. The list is
  always dense and every field always present — no nulls, or Firebase eats them.
- **Nothing is keyed by square index**, per the constraint above. *My call:*
  per-square data (owner, buildings, mortgaged) is held as fixed-length strings
  of forty characters, one character per square, the way the Tic Tac Toe board
  is a nine-character string. It survives Firebase exactly, it is trivial to
  validate in the rules file, and it diffs cleanly.
- **A turn is no longer one action.** Roll, then possibly buy, then possibly pay,
  then end. The state needs an explicit phase and an explicit record of who must
  act right now — which is not always whose turn it is, because the player paying
  rent acts during the other player's turn. `turn` alone is not enough.
- **`nextStarter` cannot be copied from the other two games.** Both derive the
  previous starter from `moves % 2`, which only works because seats strictly
  alternate every move. They do not here — doubles, multi-action turns. *My
  call:* store the starting seat in the state and add it to the rules file. The
  existing games' comments explain that they avoided storing it to dodge a rules
  change; this project is making rules changes anyway.
- **Card randomness.** `applyAction` must stay pure, so a card cannot be drawn at
  random inside it. *My call:* both decks are shuffled once into an order held in
  the state, and drawing advances a cursor — so a draw is deterministic and both
  phones agree. The shuffle happens in `createInitialState`, which is not bound
  by the purity rule. Note that `createInitialState` runs inside a
  `runTransaction` and can therefore be re-run on a retry; a reshuffle at that
  point is harmless because no one has seen a card yet, but the builder should
  satisfy themselves of that rather than take it on trust.
- **Leave room for trading.** Nothing about ownership, cash or the pending-action
  shape should assume that a change of ownership only ever happens by buying.

### Small calls, settled so the builder does not have to guess

A builder's review of this document raised five points that a reasonable person
could settle either way. Settled here, all of them my call, all of them cheap to
change if Harvey disagrees once he is playing it.

- **Houses must be built evenly** across a colour set, as in the real game. The
  supply of houses and hotels is unlimited (above), and without even-building
  that combination lets you stack five houses on one property of a set, which is
  a different game.
- **The third jail turn is not optional.** Fail to roll doubles three times and
  you pay the £50 and move. Otherwise a player with cash can sit in jail
  indefinitely, which in a two-player game is a way to refuse to lose.
- **In a tally-up, houses and hotels count at what they cost**, and an
  unmortgaged property at its purchase price. A mortgaged one counts at its
  mortgage value, as above.
- **A tally-up that comes out exactly level is a draw.** The existing games
  already model a draw (Tic Tac Toe returns `phase: 'draw'`), so the scoreboard
  and the room UI can already express it.
- **`getStatus().whoseTurn` reports whoever must act right now**, not the nominal
  turn holder. `src/main-room.js` reads only `whoseTurn` to write the turn line
  and to decide whether the board is interactive, so making it mean "it is on
  you" keeps the existing wiring honest while a player pays rent out of turn. The
  nominal turn owner stays in the state for the engine's own use.

### The board data

One plain data file, the way `src/games/snakesladders-board.js` works: data only,
no logic, imported by both the rules and the renderer so neither has to import
the other. Forty squares, their names, prices, rents, colour sets and icons, plus
the two decks.

Harvey supplies the real place names once the game is functional. Until then the
builder writes placeholders from the personal material — Keynsham to Nagpur via
Cappadocia. The card text uses their phrases ("Lovely jubbly", "Darling",
"Madame", "Jiggly Boo") as **things a card says**, not as names of squares. The
best material is the specific: Shruti asleep and not answering the call, the
balloons over both Bristol and Cappadocia, Zero Mile, saoji, biryani at the end.

*My call:* the placeholder deck text is drafted now and rewritten with Harvey in
the same pass as the place names. It is not worth two conversations.

### Rules file timing

The new `game/state` fields need `firebase-rules.json` entries, and **writes are
rejected silently until Harvey publishes them.** Tell him to publish at the point
the engine first writes state, not at the end of the project — a whole day's work
verified against a database that is quietly refusing every write is the
expensive version of this mistake.

## What is left to the builder

- The exact state encoding within the constraints above, and its validation
  entries in `firebase-rules.json`.
- The turn state machine: its phases, their names, and how an action that spans
  several steps is expressed as actions.
- Every rent table, price and card effect — the numbers.
- The renderer, the CSS, the feed's wording and how many lines it holds, and the
  animation timings.
- How the board is laid out at phone width so forty squares are legible, and
  what happens on a very small screen.
- Where the Declare bankruptcy and tally-up controls live, and their confirmation
  wording.
- Test coverage. The pure engine is exactly the kind of logic this project unit
  tests, and the bar set by the existing games is high. The renderer is verified
  by playing.
- Whether the existing `scored` / scoreboard machinery needs anything for a game
  that can end three ways (bankruptcy, tally-up, and a player simply having
  everything).

## Verifying it

Unit tests are necessary and not sufficient, and this project has the scars to
prove it. Before this is called done it is **played**: two browsers, a real room,
a game taken far enough that rent is being paid on houses, someone goes to jail
and gets out, both decks get drawn from, a property is mortgaged to cover a
rent demand, Free Parking is scooped, and the game is ended both ways —
once by declaring bankruptcy and once by tallying up.

And at least once, both tabs are closed mid-game and the game is resumed from the
rooms list, because that is half of what project one was for.
